The genotype manifests itself in a gene-expression program that is specific to each cell. DNA is transcribed into RNA, RNA is translated into proteins, and proteins in turn interact with one another, serve as structural components, catalyse the chemical reactions that produce metabolites, and bind DNA, RNA, other proteins, and metabolites to carry out diverse biological functions. Together, DNA, RNA, proteins, metabolites (including sugars and lipids), water, and other molecules form a biological network that is organised and spatially segregated into cellular organelles and cells. Each cell interacts with its environment — comprising both physical entities, such as water, extracellular proteins, the extracellular matrix, extracellular vesicles, the vasculature, and neighbouring cells, and physicochemical conditions, such as temperature, oxygen, CO2, pH, and available nutrients — to perform its functions. The subset of biological functions that can be directly observed or indirectly inferred constitutes the phenotype.

Together, genotype and environment determine which RNAs and proteins are expressed in a given cell. The interactions among these molecules, and the spatio-temporal organisation of those interactions, give rise to the phenotype. The phenotype of a cell — and of the higher-order structures it builds, such as organs and organ systems — in turn shapes the environment, most simply through the emission of CO2 and the consumption of O2. The environment then exerts pressure back on the phenotype, providing the basis for natural selection. Feedback also runs inward: the phenotype can modify the gene-expression program through epigenetic mechanisms (which change what is expressed, not the DNA sequence itself), and, over generations, it can shape the genotype through mutation and selection.

This web of interactions has a direct consequence for drug discovery. When a chemical compound modulates a biological target, typically a protein, the resulting phenotype is not determined by the compound and target alone. It emerges from the surrounding network, and therefore depends on the cell's genotype, its gene-expression program, its environment, and the interactions among all three. The same compound acting on the same target can thus induce different, and often unpredictable, phenotypes across cells, tissues, individuals, and conditions. The response propagates through and is modified by many feedback mechanisms.
